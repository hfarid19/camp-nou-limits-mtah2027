## 1. Approach in one paragraph

We never label the provided game. Instead we learn possession from **open, labelled tracking of
other games**: 7 Bundesliga matches from the DFL / IDSSE dataset, whose tracking feed carries
per-frame BallStatus and BallPossession flags that mean what the competition's ground truth means,
and 48 games of PFF FC's free 2022 World Cup data, labelled by script from PFF's own events (one
member was also fine-tuned on 20 SkillCorner A-League games, labelled by script from SkillCorner's
events). We degrade that clean tracking until it looks like the competition's automatic tracking; the
degradation's settings were fitted to label-free statistics of the provided match's tracking. We
train several sequence models on the degraded games and evaluate everything by
**leave-one-game-out** (a game is only ever scored by models that never saw it). The submission
combines four of them: a label-smoothed TCN, a wide MS-TCN, a Ball Radar-style model and a
ball-centred "possessor-pointer" network (19.3M parameters). Their outputs are decoded by **minimum-Bayes-risk (MBR)
decoding**: in each stretch of play we keep the candidate labelling with the highest expected
spellF1. The candidates include the members' majority vote under the challenge's
**controlled-possession rule** (a possession change needs controlled possession, not a touch). How
strict "controlled" is was set from independent evidence that the truth is fine-grained (Metrica's
own event-based possessions on its public sample games, DFL's flag), corroborated by the shift
scores the organisers publish in EVALUATION.md (section 2, finding 3).

## 2. What we built, and why that rather than something else

**The original pipeline.** It used hand-written per-frame evidence (nearest player to the ball,
team shape, ball gaps, Smart Tagging) decoded with a 3-state Viterbi (H / A / D). It scored only
0.36 spellF1 on our DFL bench. The hand-set evidence was the weak part, so we replaced it with
learned evidence.

**Benches.** All candidates implement one interface and are scored by `candidates/bench.py`.
The primary bench is the 7 DFL games, leave-one-game-out. The bench refuses to run if a model
trained on the scored game is loaded. Secondary benches: Metrica's open sample games, the 20-game
SkillCorner A-League set, and PFF out-of-fold (a screen only; its scores are inflated, see
section 6). Three findings shaped the bench:

1. **Our first degradation simulator was wrong.** It hid the ball in many short random bursts
   during play. Label-free statistics of the provided match (`candidates/match_stats.py`) show
   something else. The real ball is smooth and missing almost only in ~127 long gaps per 90 min
   (median 10.7 s). During those gaps the players move exactly like DFL players at dead ball
   (median team speed 1.0 m/s vs 1.02 at DFL dead ball and 2.27 in live play). We rebuilt the
   simulator (`degrade_v2.py`), fitted its settings to those statistics (`fit_degrade_v2.py`),
   and scored with 3 seeds per game (bench v2).
2. **DFL's possession flag flips on every touch; the competition's does not.** CHALLENGE.md
   counts a change of possession only when the opponent gains *controlled* possession. A touch,
   a deflection or an aerial duel without control does not count, and the suggested proxy is
   "a player keeps the ball for a few seconds". We therefore score against DFL labels
   with that rule applied (`candidates/control.py`): opponent spells shorter than T seconds are
   folded back into the surrounding possession. T is unknown. We first selected on the mean over
   T = 1, 2, 3 s (`ctrl_mean`).
3. **The truth counts possessions finely: T is about 1–1.5 s, not 2–3 s.** The evidence, in the
   order we weigh it:
   - **Metrica's own event-based possessions.** Metrica co-organises the challenge and produced its
     tracking and Smart Tagging. Building possessions from Metrica's event tagging of its three
     public sample games (`candidates/control_events/metrica_rules.py`) gives 305–331 possessions
     per 90, with 17–22% of spells under 2 s. That implies T of about 1.2–1.5 s.
   - **DFL's own flag.** On our DFL bench the raw flag gives about 412 possessions per 90, a 1 s
     fold 345, 1.5 s 303 and 2 s 272. Metrica's count sits between the 1 s and 1.5 s folds. Our
     2 s target was well outside that range.
   - **Other providers' labels.** PFF's and SkillCorner's possession labels, built by script from
     their own events, give 314 and 279 possessions per 90: the same range, far finer than a 2 s
     fold.
   - **Corroboration from the organisers' documentation.** EVALUATION.md publishes the full-match
     spellF1 of a perfect submission shifted 3 / 8 / 25 frames late: 0.982 / 0.893 / 0.654. These
     numbers depend only on how long the true possessions are, so they summarise the full-match
     truth's possession lengths. We used them as published, alongside the evidence above. On DFL,
     the raw flag gives 0.960 / 0.862 / 0.557, a 1 s fold 0.998 / 0.958 / 0.663, and our earlier
     2 s target 0.999 / 0.989 / 0.797. Metrica's event-based possessions give 0.94 / 0.88 / 0.66,
     PFF's 0.977 / 0.902 / 0.632 and SkillCorner's 0.980 / 0.897 / 0.663. The published scores sit
     between the raw DFL flag and a 1 s fold, close to the other providers' labels and far from
     the 2 s target, so they point the same way as the independent evidence.

   From 2026-11-09 we select on **fine4**: the mean spellF1 against the raw flag, the 0.5 s and
   1 s folds, and event labels with a 1 s hold (`candidates/score_sel2.py`).

**Models we built and kept.** The member families started as models trained only on degraded DFL
games (`*_v2`, then `*_v3` after the data fix below). From v6 on, every member (retrained or new) is
trained on the 7 DFL + 48 PFF World Cup games, with leave-one-game-out fold
models on DFL and game-grouped out-of-fold models on PFF. In F4sP the MS-TCN was then fine-tuned
with the 20 SkillCorner A-League games added (its DFL fold models too, so DFL stays leave-one-game-out).

| model | what it is | role in the submission |
|---|---|---|

| `handover_mstcn_v2/v3` → `nn_pff` | MS-TCN with an ASRF boundary branch (action-segmentation literature): per-frame class plus boundary probability, segmented at boundary peaks | **member 2** as `m5c_C192ls_skcft`: width 192 with label smoothing 0.05 (`m5c_C192ls`, F4b's member), fine-tuned at a low learning rate with the 20 SkillCorner games added (`mstcn_c192ls_skcft.npz`) |

**MBR decoding** (`candidates/mbr_decode`). For each game, the candidates are complete labellings:
the members' hard vote under v6's rule (the base), the vote under the 11 other combinations of 1 s
smoothing on/off and fold 0–1.5 s, and each member alone, raw or under v6's rule. We draw 32
posterior samples from a 3-label hidden Markov model on the members' mean probabilities (4
sub-states per label, so durations are negative-binomial; fixed seed). Each sample is read at
folds of 0, 0.5 and 1 s, the fine granularities of section 2. Between frames where every candidate
says dead ball, we keep the candidate with the highest expected spellF1 against the samples.
spellF1 is a ratio, so this is solved by iterating on its linearisation. The decoder's settings
come from a 21-configuration grid fixed before any result. They were chosen nested on DFL (each
game scored with the settings that were best on the other six) and the full-DFL pick for the
member set is shipped: alpha 0.1, rho 0.2, margin 0 for F4sP (the same pick in all 7 nested folds;
F4b's members had picked alpha 1).

**A training-data bug, found late.** Every member's training set took each game's attack
direction from the home team's mean x over the first half. For one of the 7 DFL games
(J03WR9) that is wrong: the home team kicks off from the left but spends the half in the right
half, so every direction-dependent feature of that game was mirrored. The bench and the match
were never affected (the bench uses the kick-off positions, the match its metadata). Retraining
the three members on corrected data (`*_v3`, otherwise unchanged recipes) gains +0.008 on the
DFL bench, +0.006 on the 64 PFF games, and +0.012 on the Metrica holdout.

**Combination.** A vote over all 11 early candidates was *worse* than the best single model. Seven
of them share the same LightGBM evidence and make correlated errors (correlation 0.81-0.91), so
they outvote the diverse ones. Nested leave-one-game-out selection picked exactly the three
diverse models. Bigger votes stayed no better in round 4 (section 6). What helped in round 5 was
a better combiner (MBR) and a genuinely different fourth model (the pointer).

## 3. Assets used — and dropped

**Used.**
- **Tactical tracking (ATD):** players and ball, the main input.
- **Smart Tagging:** small priors in the members' decoders. D before each half's kick-off tag, a
  dead-ball nudge on the long ball gap at a goal tag, and a ±0.4 log-odds team prior from
  build-up / progression phases. FAQ.md confirms it may be used. These priors were set by hand
  once and **are not validated**: none of our benches has Smart Tagging, so they act only on the
  match. Their effect is small.
- **Homography and video:** not used by the submission. The tactical tracking is already in pitch
  coordinates. The match video was used in one dropped experiment (the video touch detector
  below), run locally.

## 5. The possession rules we implemented

- **Dead vs alive.** D wherever the model believes the ball is out of play, following DFL's
  BallStatus. We do not predict who "owns" a dead ball (CHALLENGE.md).
- **Loose balls and passes in flight** stay with the last team. That is DFL's BallPossession
  semantics, which our models learned.
- **Controlled possession.** In v6, a predicted opponent spell shorter than 1.25 s that directly
  follows the other team's possession is handed back to that team: a touch or deflection without
  control does not change possession. In F4b and F4sP that rule defines the base candidate of the MBR
  decoder, which may depart from it in a stretch of play when another candidate (another
  smoothing/fold rule, or a single member) has a higher expected spellF1 at fine granularity
  (folds of 0–1 s).
  - **How 1.25 s was chosen.** Nested leave-one-game-out on fine4 picked it for v4, together with
    raw-flag training targets (6 of 7 folds). When the members were retrained on PFF (v6), nested
    selection on DFL fine4 + PFF out-of-fold fine3 preferred a shorter rule (no smoothing, 0.75 s
    fold). That rule lost on
    SkillCorner (fine3 −0.0073 ± 0.0040), so v6 kept 1.25 s under a decision rule written before
    the next results were seen. With v6's members, DFL alone prefers the 1 s merge with no further
    fold (fine4 0.7328 vs 0.7289), while SkillCorner prefers 1.5 s (0.6018 vs 0.5964). 1.25 s is a
    hedge between the two benches, not the optimum of either.
  - A longer threshold (2 s, our first choice) fits event-defined labels with a 3 s hold, but
    those labels count possessions much more coarsely than the evidence in section 2.
- **Before the kick-off** every frame is dead (FAQ.md: the second half is graded from 110,808 but
  kicks off at 113,742). The kick is placed 88 frames after the half's "Kick Off (Start)" tag, the
  lag seen at the first-half kick-off.
- **After a stoppage** the first possession is kept even if short: a restart always has a clear
  possessor.
- **Possessions are maximal runs of one team with the ball alive.** A stoppage always splits them.

### 6.2 Rounds 4 and 5: from v6 to F4b, then F4sP

**Decision rules, written before results.** The rule files are copied verbatim in `docs/prereg/`.
Round 4 (9 Nov, `docs/prereg/round4_prereg.txt`) made a candidate
eligible if, paired against v6, it gained ≥ +0.005 and ≥ 2 SE on DFL fine4, was not worse by
more than 1 SE on SkillCorner fine3 (20 games) and not worse by more than 0.010 on Metrica dev. The pick was the
eligible candidate with the highest DFL gain, to be confirmed once on 13 FOOTPASS holdout games.
Round 5 (9 Nov evening, `docs/prereg/round5_prereg.txt`) kept these gates, added a robustness route for
domain-shift methods, and changed the pick to the highest DFL + SkillCorner gain sum, with the
label-smoothed vote "lsw" as the default unless beaten by > 1 SE. That change was made **while
round 4 was running and after some of its DFL results had been seen**. It is more conservative
than the round-4 rule, but it was a mid-stream change. After an independent review showed that
SkillCorner rewards coarser output and DFL punishes it, an addendum required a SkillCorner gain
claimed under the robustness route (b) to hold at a matched possession count (v6 re-folded per game
to the candidate's count). The addendum applies to route (b) only; F4b qualifies under route (a),
the DFL route (it also passes (b), matched count included).
Decoder settings had to be chosen nested on DFL only.

**What was tried.**
- *Round 4* (members, on rented GPUs): label smoothing, width and context for the MS-TCN, an
  MS-TCN scaling ladder, 8 degradation seeds, 64-game PFF retrains, 5–7 member votes, Temba /
  ASFormer and full-attention transformers. Best outcome: the MS-TCN at width 192 with label
  smoothing (`m5c_C192ls`; alone vs the previous wide MS-TCN: DFL +0.0090, SkillCorner +0.0171).
  The 8-seed retrains reproduced the label-smoothed vote's gain (+0.0107 vs +0.0099), which
  suggests that gain comes from the recipe, not one lucky training run. The rest is in the
  dropped table (section 3).
- *Round 5*: MBR decoding; label-free adaptation (per-game re-normalisation, test-time
  averaging, noisy students); new architectures (the possessor pointer, auxiliary targets,
  self-supervised pretraining, a segment head, on-the-fly degradation, random search).
  - MBR on the lsw members: DFL +0.0137 ± 0.0044 vs v6 (nested), SkillCorner +0.0123 ± 0.0033
    (+0.0100 at matched count), Metrica +0.009.
  - Adding test-time averaging and a = 0.5 re-normalisation to the TCN under MBR: DFL
    +0.0149 ± 0.0038, SkillCorner +0.0206 (+0.0169 matched). Applying the averaging to the TCN
    only, and combining it with re-normalisation, were decided after seeing the both-members
    result, so this part is post hoc.
  - A cautionary result: a decoder setting chosen nested on DFL from a larger, exploratory grid
    gained on DFL (+0.0146) but lost on SkillCorner (−0.0070). We kept the grid that was fixed
    before any result (g1); that ruling was made after both grids' SkillCorner scores were known.

**Multiplicity.** By the time the final recipe was written down, we had compared 74 vote sets,
72 decoder variants, about 40 adaptation variants (41 distinct names in the R5-2 result logs,
member-alone diagnostics included) and 49 architecture screens. At that scale the ranking among
the top rows of the table is noise. So the final was not chosen by its rank in the table: it was
**built slot by slot under a recipe** (`docs/prereg/round5_prereg.txt`, addendum of 10 Nov 01:32),
written before the pointer's SkillCorner result and before any ensemble containing the pointer or
the on-the-fly MS-TCN was scored:
- TCN slot: `o_ls05` with test-time averaging and a = 0.5 re-normalisation (fixed settings);
- MS-TCN slot: the member with the highest DFL + SkillCorner gain alone among three 48-game
  candidates (`m5c_C192ls`; 64-game models excluded to keep the spent PFF holdout out of the
  weights);
- radar slot: `ball_radar_pff`;
- pointer slot: only if the pointer, alone, is not worse than the TCN alone on SkillCorner by more
  than 1 SE;
- decoder: MBR, settings chosen nested per DFL game from the fixed grid.

The recipe was not written blind, and it protects less than "built from confirmed parts" suggests:
- F3's exact member set had already been scored, with fixed decoder settings, 11 minutes before
  the addendum (`mbrtta4a50_C192`, 01:21: DFL +0.0178, SkillCorner +0.0209 vs v6), and it was among
  the top rows of the table (fifth of 74 by DFL + SkillCorner gain).
- The MS-TCN slot's deciding member-alone result was already logged (`m5c_C192ls` vs `m5c_W`: DFL
  +0.0090, SkillCorner +0.0171, at 01:02).
- Only the MS-TCN and pointer slots had member-level gates. The TCN slot copied the settings of the
  then-leading pipeline (averaging on the TCN only, plus a = 0.5), which were themselves chosen
  post hoc (above); the radar slot had no test.

What the recipe did fix in advance was the pointer decision and the decoder procedure.

The first pointer failed its gate, so the pick became **F3** (no pointer). A second pointer
variant, `r5_ptr_ballc` (inputs centred on the ball), was then admitted under the same gate by an

before its SkillCorner and Metrica results and before any ensemble containing it was scored. By
then, though, a 4-member MBR ensemble with the first pointer (TCN with averaging and a = 0.5, the
wide MS-TCN, radar, pointer) had been scored at 01:51 (DFL +0.0231, SkillCorner +0.0191 vs v6,

- Any plausible gain on the hidden match is well below the game-to-game spread of scores (SD about

### 6.3 What the models learned, in rules a coach can read

Two interpretable models trained the same way show what the bigger models rely on
(`candidates/archive/decision_tree_b`, scoring 0.654 vs the depth-12 tree's 0.666, and
`decision_tree_a`, 0.694):

- **Dead ball:** the ball is untracked or has moved ≤ 0.58 m in half a second, nobody has
  controlled it for over 3 s, and the players average ≤ 2.1 m/s → dead (97% of 41% of frames).
  A ball outside the pitch is dead even right after a touch.
- **Which team:** the team that last had a player alone on the ball keeps it (86% on held-out
  games). Confidence grows when that control was recent and its nearest player is clearly
  closer to the ball than the opponent's. The single most useful summary is "which team was
  near the ball over the last 3 s", the top split of the better tree. For the score itself, the
  ball's direction of travel matters most. The pointer member, which looks only at the players
  nearest the ball, is the best single model on the DFL bench (the 0.8M version on SkillCorner only
  ties the TCN alone, +0.0017 ± 0.0043, 10/20), which fits this picture.

How far we came, on the DFL leave-one-game-out bench (v1 degradation, raw labels): original
pipeline 0.360 → learned evidence 0.624 → sequence models 0.654 → majority vote 0.662. On the
final bench against DFL's own flag: original 0.370 → v6 0.693. On fine4: v2 0.662 → v6 0.729 →
F4b 0.756 → F4sP 0.762.

## 7. Limitations

- **The benches are proxies.** Both the degradation and the controlled-possession threshold are
  inferred, not observed. We fitted the first to the match's label-free statistics. The second
  rests on Metrica's and DFL's possession granularity, corroborated by the published shift
  scores. If the truth were coarser after all (a 2 s reading), our move to fine-grained output in
  round 2 would cost about 0.04. MBR decoding also leans slightly finer than lsw: −0.009 at a
  1.5 s reading, measured for MBR on the lsw members and not recomputed for F4b or F4sP.
- **Design reuse of a small bench.** Weights never see the game they are scored on, but for three
  weeks every recipe, rule and member choice was made on the same 7 DFL games, and later also on
  SkillCorner. Only the 16 PFF holdout games and Metrica Game 3 (both for v6) and the 13 FOOTPASS
  games (for F4b) were scored once. F4sP, the submission, has no such once-scored confirmation, and
  SkillCorner is also training data for its MS-TCN (its SkillCorner scores are cross-validated).
- **Short and contested possessions.** Measured for lsw (F4b's predecessor; not recomputed for
  F4b or F4sP) against the DFL flag folded at 1 s, at IoU 0.5: 26.6% of true possessions are missed.
  The causes, as shares of true possessions: short ones under 2 s 8.2%, boundaries off 6.3%,
  wrong team 4.4%, merged or split 4.2%, predicted dead 3.4%. 17.1% of predicted possessions are
  unmatched: split or boundary 7.4%, wrong team 6.0%, spurious short 3.6%. Genuine quick
  interceptions are still lost.
- **Only 7 professionally labelled games**, all from one league and one tracking system. The PFF,
  SkillCorner and FOOTPASS labels are our own scripts on other providers' events.
- **The match looks different from our training games.** A classifier tells the match apart from
  every DFL game (AUC 0.99), mostly through player tracking. The real tracker breaks player tracks
  in crowds (62% of track ends vs 16% in our simulator); a simulator that does the same
  (`degrade_v3`) does not lower our scores. Models disagree more on the match than on the bench:
  the v2 members on 6.3% of frames vs 3-4% (mostly dead vs live flickers while the ball is outside
  the pitch lines); v6 and lsw on 1.8% vs 1.2%. Expect the real score to be lower than the
  bench's.
- **Smart Tagging priors are unvalidated** (section 3).
- **A large pointer for a small gain.** F4sP's pointer has 19.3M parameters (77.1 MB of weights,
  93 MB for all four members). Its forward pass now runs in PyTorch on the CPU in float64
  (83 s for the match on one thread of a moderately loaded Apple M5 Max; the numpy forward pass it replaces took about 24 minutes alone and
  made the whole run 54 minutes on a heavily shared Apple M5 Max; F4b took about 4-7 minutes). The
  submission therefore needs torch (CPU wheel) installed to run in minutes; without it the numpy
  path gives the same file, slowly (section 8). Its bench probabilities came from a float32
  GPU port, checked against the numpy path on two sample games and on the live = offline
  games of `candidates/final_f4sp/README.md`, not on every bench game.
- **Statistical resolution.** With 7 games we can only resolve differences of about 0.005-0.016,
  and per-frame statistics overstate the evidence roughly a hundredfold, because a dead-ball state
  lasts about 31 s. One match is a single noisy draw.
- **Reproducibility.** The CSV is reproduced byte-identically from the committed weights on the
  platforms listed in section 8. Native x86-64 hardware was not tested, only x86-64 under
  emulation. Training (torch, GPU) is not bit-reproducible; retraining gives slightly different
  weights.
- **With more time:** a touch-level detector good enough to tell controlled receptions from
  deflections (our simulation says it needs team accuracy ≥ 0.95 and precision/recall ≥ 0.9 to
  help); more professionally labelled leagues; and checking the degradation model against a second
  competition-style feed.

## 8. Reproduction

```bash
# 1. data: unpack the bundle into data/ (DATA.md)
# 2. environment (Python 3.14.6; installs the CPU-only torch wheel from the PyTorch index on Linux)
pip install -r requirements-submission.txt
# 3. the submission file (byte-identical, single thread, a few minutes)
python make_submission.py            # -> evaluation/results/camp_nou_limits.csv
python evaluation/validate_submission.py evaluation/results/camp_nou_limits.csv
```

Expected sha256:
`60fc793e90f1df6ce5dda4b2140988fdfd1c1cfc4ee01950b10c429186dd6c57` (326 possessions, dead share
0.457).

The pointer's network forward pass has two backends that give this same file: PyTorch on the CPU in
float64 (`candidates/nn_pointer/ptr_torch.py`, the default when torch is installed; it runs in a
worker process, because torch and LightGBM in one process hang or crash on macOS) and numpy in
float64 (`ptr_np.py`, the reference, used when torch is missing or with `MTAH_PTR_BACKEND=numpy`).
The torch port does the numpy code's operations in the same order (convolutions as matrix products,
oneDNN disabled, deterministic algorithms on); on the match its probabilities differ from numpy's by
at most 2.4e-15 and are identical after the float32 rounding that precedes the decoder.

We obtained exactly this file
- with the torch backend (PyTorch 2.15.0):
  - macOS arm64 (Apple Accelerate): `make_submission.py` (which always pins one thread), and
    `candidates/final_f4sp/match_run.py`, the same pipeline without the pin, with 1, 2 and 8
    numpy and torch threads;
  - Linux arm64 in a container (OpenBLAS, torch 2.15.0+cpu);
  - Linux x86-64 in a container under Rosetta emulation (torch 2.15.0+cpu);
  - a fresh clone of the repository in a Linux arm64 container with only
    `requirements-submission.txt` installed;
- with the numpy backend, when v8 was built: macOS arm64 (`make_submission.py`; `match_run.py`
  unpinned twice and with `OMP_NUM_THREADS` = 2 and 8), Linux arm64 (working tree and a fresh
  copy) and Linux x86-64 under Rosetta emulation, all in containers; and again on macOS with
  `MTAH_PTR_BACKEND=numpy` after the torch backend was added (30 minutes).

The containers were `python:3.14.6-slim`, which also needs the system OpenMP runtime for LightGBM
(`apt-get install libgomp1`). On Linux, `requirements-submission.txt` takes torch from
`https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cpu` (its `--extra-index-url` line), which gives the CPU-only
2.15.0+cpu wheel instead of PyPI's CUDA build. Perturbing the member probabilities fed to the decoder
by up to a relative 1e-6 (3 random draws), or rounding them to 1e-6, changes no output frame (checked
with both backends). Native x86-64 hardware was not tested.

Run time with torch: `make_submission.py` took 3 min 24 s, 5 min 28 s and 9 min 54 s on one thread
of the same Apple M5 Max, which other jobs shared throughout (load averages about 5, 8 and 9; the
slowest run overlapped other jobs' heavy numerical work); the pointer's forward pass alone took
83 s in a comparison run. The Linux containers (6 virtual CPUs on that Mac, also shared) took
15-16 minutes on arm64 and 24 minutes on x86-64 under Rosetta emulation. With numpy,
`make_submission.py` took 54 minutes (3230 s; 2757 s of CPU) on one thread of an Apple M5 Max that
other jobs kept heavily loaded (load average about 35 on 18 cores), and 64-66 minutes in the Linux
arm64 containers on the same machine; the pointer was over 90% of it.

Weights files over GitHub's 100 MB limit can be stored as numbered parts with
`python candidates/chunked_weights.py split <file.npz>` (writes `<file.npz>.part000`, `.part001`,
... and `<file.npz>.sha256`); the pointer and MS-TCN loaders, and the benches' fold-model lookup,
read the parts (joined in memory and checked against the sha256) when the single file is absent.
No shipped file needs this yet.

The submission loads committed model files only (about 93 MB for the four members, 77 MB of it the
pointer), needs no network, and never touches the ground truth. Rebuilding the models from the open
data, and every bench number in this document, is described in `candidates/README.md` and each
candidate's README (`candidates/final_f4sp/README.md` lists F4sP's parts,
`candidates/final_f4b/README.md` F4b's). The SkillCorner A-League data for the MS-TCN's fine-tune
comes from github.com/SkillCorner/opendata. That needs `requirements-train.txt` (the
submission's pins, torch included; training ran on GPUs), the IDSSE download script (`candidates/learned_dfl_evidence/fetch_idsse.py`), the PFF
World Cup release, and the work directory `$MTAH_WORK`. FOOTPASS is not needed to rebuild
anything that ships.
